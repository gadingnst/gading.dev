A few days ago someone commented on one of my posts on X, translated from Indonesian: *"go on then, let me plagiarize it, if I even can."*

I laughed. Then I quoted the comment and replied: there's no such thing as a moat in this era, everything can be copied. What makes the difference is the thinking behind a product, and where it's being driven.

![The comment I quoted, and my reply about moats and forks](/media/blog/ai-clone-is-just-a-fork/tweet.jpg)

But afterwards the question got stuck in my head: if any product can be cloned now, what actually makes yours different? A reply that short can't really answer it.

This post is my answer, the version that doesn't fit in a tweet.

## Everything can be replicated, and that's not an exaggeration

Look at how it works now. Give an AI a screenshot of a product, or a link to its website, type "build me something like this", and wait. The code comes out, the UI comes out, the main features come out. If the product is mostly screens and CRUD, the result is close enough to make its founder break into a cold sweat.

Copying someone's product used to take a team, months of work and an understanding of the domain. That cost has collapsed. So if your moat was "nobody has built this yet" or "it's a pain to build", sorry, that moat is gone.

That doesn't make every product worthless, though. This pattern isn't new. Developers have lived with it for years.

## Developers have known this pattern for years

It's called a fork.

You find a good open source repo, fork it, tweak a few things to fit what you need. Then you stop. A few months later your fork is stale. The original keeps moving: bugs get fixed, features land, dependencies get bumped. Your fork falls hundreds of commits behind, and the further it falls the less you want to sync it, because every sync means more conflicts.

I'm pretty sure most developers have a fork like that on their GitHub account. While writing this I went through mine, and found a perfect example.

## My own stale fork

In 2022 I forked [Umami](https://github.com/umami-software/umami), an open source web analytics tool, to use on this site. To be honest, I didn't fork it to take Umami in a different direction. I just wanted an analytics tool with my own name on it. I named the repo `analytics.gading.dev`, rebranded it as "Gadings Analytics", swapped the favicon and deployed it. Done.

In June 2023 I synced it with upstream once while doing another round of rebranding. After that? Never touched it again.

![My Umami fork, 4,545 commits behind](/media/blog/ai-clone-is-just-a-fork/fork-stale.jpg)

When I checked today, my fork was still stuck on version 2.2.0. Upstream is on v3.5.0, released on 12 November 2026. Side by side, my fork has just 16 commits upstream doesn't, and is **4,545 commits behind**.

And here's the problem. All I took was the code and the name. I had no spark for the product, so I never developed it. But software is alive, you could say, because its development keeps going and never stops. My fork died the day I stopped touching it.

The funny part is that I don't use that fork anymore. I went back to using Umami itself, no fork, so I can keep up with its updates. The `analytics.gading.dev` domain now returns a 404. I was so keen on having my own version, and I ended up as an upstream user again anyway.

I think that's what will happen to most people who like to clone things with AI. The motive is the same: wanting a product with your own name on it. The code can be taken, but the spark doesn't get copied.

## Cloning with AI is the same idea as a fork

Now picture the same thing for a commercial product.

The only difference is how you get the copy. Forking is for open source: you click the fork button, tweak a bit, done. A commercial product's code isn't open, so you couldn't fork it before. AI opens that door. A screenshot and a link are now enough to act as the "fork button".

The result is still the same, though. What you get from cloning with AI is a snapshot of the product as it is today. The code can be copied, the UI can be copied, the feature flow can be copied. The mind that built the product doesn't come with it.

![The original keeps shipping, the clone stops at one point](/media/blog/ai-clone-is-just-a-fork/clone-snapshot.jpg)

So from here, there are two things I don't think can ever be cloned.

### 1. How the product owner thinks behind every decision

A screenshot only shows what's on the screen. It doesn't show why the product is shaped the way it is, or what was deliberately not chosen.

Here's an example from what I'm working on right now, [Nouride](https://nouride.com). It's an AI agent daemon that runs on a small box you own. From the outside it looks like "an AI bot you can chat with from Telegram, Discord and WhatsApp". What you can't see are the decisions behind it:

- **One daemon, one binary.** Every agent, the chat gateways, the dashboard and the scheduled jobs all run in one process from a single executable. No microservice stack, no five containers to start. While it waits, it uses about 64 MB of memory and under 1% of one CPU core. It's built for a small box at home: a mini PC, a Raspberry Pi, even a second-hand set-top box.
- **As few external dependencies as possible.** The daemon uses just 5 outside packages. LLM calls go through plain `fetch()`, not the OpenAI or Anthropic SDKs. The database is SQLite, one file, not Postgres. The reason: every package you add is one more door for a supply chain attack, and one more thing to maintain.
- **Your data stays on your box.** Conversations, memory and API keys are stored on your own machine, the keys encrypted. What leaves is only what an agent sends to the models and services you picked.

Someone cloning Nouride would see the features, but wouldn't know any of that was deliberate. They might even split it into several services and add Postgres, Redis and SDKs everywhere because it looks "more serious" or "more enterprise". The result would look similar on the surface but could no longer run light on a small box. And that's the whole point of the product.

### 2. Where the product is going

A clone only knows the product as it is today. It doesn't know next week's plan, which problem is being chased, or which user feedback is being prioritised.

Tomorrow the original ships something new and the clone is behind again. Exactly like my Umami fork that never got synced.

## When forks do win

To be fair, forks don't always lose. Open source history has a few forks that overtook the original. But look closer and those examples actually back up my argument.

**LibreOffice.** In September 2010, most of the outside OpenOffice.org developers walked out over how Sun, and then Oracle, ran the project. They formed The Document Foundation and released LibreOffice in January 2011. Most Linux distributions moved over soon after. In April 2011, Oracle stopped developing OpenOffice.org and later handed the code to Apache. Apache OpenOffice still exists, but its last significant feature release was 4.1 in 2014, and it has struggled for years to keep enough active contributors.

**io.js.** In December 2014, a group of Node.js contributors forked it as io.js over governance problems under Joyent. Commits to Node.js itself had been falling through 2014. io.js released far more often and kept up with the latest V8. In the end, in September 2015, the two projects merged back as Node.js v4.0 under the Node.js Foundation, with the codebase taken from the io.js repository.

The pattern is the same. The fork didn't win because it copied the code. It won because **the people making the decisions moved to the fork**, while the upstream stopped moving. What moved was the minds, not just the code. The spark moved with them.

An AI clone doesn't carry that. It only carries the code. So the only way a clone overtakes you is if you're the one who stops moving.

## So how do you stay the upstream

Instead of worrying about being cloned, put that energy into being the upstream: the repo that keeps moving and leaves its forks behind. In practice, for me, that means:

- **Keep shipping, and make yourself and your product visible.** A public changelog, release notes, progress people can follow. A clone can copy your product today, but people follow the one that keeps moving.
- **Write down the reasons behind your decisions.** Why this feature exists, why that one was left out on purpose. That can't be cloned from a screenshot, and it helps people understand where your product is going.
- **Listen to users, and reply fast.** User feedback is input only you get. A clone has no relationship with your users at all.
- **Don't stop.** This is the one that matters most. The moment you stop, you're the stale fork, and the clone just catches up.

Clones will always exist, and they'll keep getting cheaper. But as long as you keep making decisions and shipping, whoever clones you will always be chasing yesterday's version.
